# Block 01 — Skeletal Muscle: contraction -> force -> adaptation

Status: **MOSTLY CONSOLIDATED / remaining mechanistic uncertainties**  
Research passes: 2026-09-12, 2026-09-13

## 1. Excitation-contraction coupling

### Operational model
Motor-neuron action potential -> acetylcholine at neuromuscular junction -> muscle action potential -> propagation through sarcolemma and T-tubules -> CaV1.1/DHPR activation -> RyR1 opening in sarcoplasmic reticulum -> cytosolic Ca2+ rise -> troponin C binding -> tropomyosin displacement -> actin-myosin cross-bridge cycling -> force. Relaxation depends mainly on Ca2+ resequestration by SERCA.

### Why this matters for the coach
Force loss can arise upstream or downstream of the contractile machinery: neural drive, excitation-contraction coupling, metabolite accumulation, substrate depletion and structural/tissue factors are not interchangeable. Avoid the vague label “muscle fatigue” when a more specific mechanism is relevant.

Confidence: **HIGH** for the physiological sequence; **LOW-MODERATE** for inferring a specific molecular failure from consumer-level symptoms or wearable data.

## 2. Hypertrophy is not adequately modeled as “damage -> repair -> growth”

Mechanical loading is the central external stimulus for resistance-training hypertrophy, translated through mechanosensitive signaling networks that interact with mTORC1, translational capacity/ribosomal biogenesis, muscle protein synthesis, satellite cells and myonuclear accretion. Muscle damage may occur but should not be treated as a required driver or a useful target.

### Decision rule
Do not prescribe soreness or muscle damage as a goal. Optimize repeated high-quality loading that can be recovered from and progressed.

Confidence: **HIGH** that progressive mechanical loading drives hypertrophy; **MODERATE** regarding the exact relative contribution of individual mechanosensors/signaling pathways in humans.

Key reference: Lim C et al. *An Evidence-Based Narrative Review of Mechanisms of Resistance Exercise-Induced Human Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy.* Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2022. PMID 35389932.

## 3. Mechanotransduction: useful model, not a single solved sensor

Candidate load-sensing systems include costameric/integrin-focal adhesion signaling, FAK-associated pathways, phosphatidic-acid signaling, titin-linked signaling, cytoskeletal proteins such as filamin-C/BAG3 and Hippo-pathway effectors YAP/TAZ. These pathways can converge on mTORC1-dependent and mTORC1-independent control of protein synthesis, transcription and remodeling.

The important limitation is that the relative in-vivo importance of each mechanosensor in exercising humans is not established well enough to prescribe training from one molecular pathway.

### Skill guardrail
Use mechanosensors to explain biological plausibility. Do **not** infer that an exercise is superior because it theoretically produces more activation of FAK, YAP/TAZ, titin signaling or another isolated pathway unless longitudinal human outcomes support the claim.

Confidence: **MODERATE** for the network model; **LOW-MODERATE** for ranking individual mechanosensors in humans.

References: Lim et al. PMID 35389932; integrin review PMID 31314586; titin review PMID 26973541. Much direct causal work remains preclinical.

## 4. Acute molecular responses are not validated surrogates for long-term outcomes

A transient rise in mTORC1 signaling, gene expression or post-exercise muscle protein synthesis does not by itself prove that an intervention causes superior long-term hypertrophy or performance. Acute mechanistic evidence is useful for plausibility, not sufficient for prescription.

### Skill guardrail
Any recommendation based primarily on an acute biomarker must be labeled **mechanistic/provisional** until supported by longitudinal human outcomes.

Confidence: **HIGH** as an evidence-interpretation rule.

## 5. Resistance-training prescription — 2026 ACSM synthesis

The 2026 ACSM Position Stand is an overview of systematic reviews of randomized resistance-training trials in healthy adults and updates the 2009 guidance. Eligible interventions lasted at least 6 weeks. It supports resistance training as effective for muscle function and hypertrophy while showing that the optimal prescription depends on the target outcome rather than one universal program.

Operational translation currently supported for the skill:
- Strength benefits especially from heavier loading and task-specific practice.
- Hypertrophy can occur across a broad loading range when sets provide sufficient effort and volume.
- Weekly volume is an important hypertrophy variable, but the dose-response is not infinite and individual tolerance matters.
- Many advanced methods are optional tools rather than mandatory ingredients.

Reference: Currier BS et al. *American College of Sports Medicine Position Stand. Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews.* Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2026. PMID 41843416. DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897.

Confidence: **HIGH** for broad principles; exact thresholds should be stored with outcome/population context instead of universalized.

## 6. Failure vs non-failure

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses do not support momentary muscular failure as necessary for hypertrophy. A 2022 meta-analysis found no superiority of momentary failure over non-failure for hypertrophy. A 2026 meta-analysis of 20 studies / 556 participants reported no significant hypertrophy advantage for failure and a small advantage for non-failure training in dynamic strength.

A resistance-trained trial comparing momentary failure with ~2 repetitions in reserve found appreciable adaptations in both conditions, with generally modest between-condition differences.

### Decision rule
Treat proximity to failure as a continuous dose/fatigue variable, not a binary doctrine. Select it based on load, exercise safety, volume, athlete skill, fatigue cost and recovery context.

### Monitoring
Record RIR/RPE at least for representative work sets if the value will be used to regulate progression. Do not assume RIR estimates are equally accurate across exercises or users; calibrate them against repeated performance when useful.

References: Refalo et al. PMID 36334240; Grgic et al. PMID 33497853; 2026 meta-analysis PMID 42410632; resistance-trained trial PMID 38393985; *Without Fail* PMID 40249908.

Confidence: **HIGH** that failure is not required; **MODERATE** for the optimal RIR by exercise/person because direct dose-response data remain limited.

## 7. Ribosomal biogenesis / translational capacity

Resistance training can increase ribosome-related transcription and total RNA, expanding translational capacity. In a contralateral 12-week human study, the greater hypertrophy seen with higher training volume was associated at the individual level with greater early ribosomal biogenesis. This supports ribosome content as a plausible mediator of adaptation, not as a routine coaching biomarker.

Importantly, baseline genomic rDNA copy number did **not** predict training-induced hypertrophy in a 10-12 week cohort of young men and women. Acute markers of ribosomal biogenesis rose after training despite no relationship between rDNA copy number and growth.

### Decision rule
Use ribosomal biogenesis to understand why sustained training may expand protein-synthetic capacity. Do not genotype or measure rDNA copy number to predict a person's hypertrophy response in routine coaching.

Evidence: human longitudinal/biopsy studies. References: PMID 31813190; PMID 39005083.

Confidence: **MODERATE** that ribosomal expansion contributes to hypertrophy; **HIGH** that rDNA copy number is not currently a useful practical predictor.

## 8. Satellite cells and myonuclear accretion

Resistance exercise can expand the Pax7+ satellite-cell pool. A systematic review/meta-analysis found an increase in muscle Pax7+ cells after resistance exercise, with roughly ~50% higher content than baseline during 24-72 h in pooled acute data. Longitudinal human studies show increases in satellite cells and myonuclei during resistance training, and these changes correlate with fiber hypertrophy.

However, correlation is not proof that myonuclear accretion is required for every increment of hypertrophy. Animal and human literature indicates hypertrophy can begin without immediate satellite-cell fusion, while larger or sustained remodeling appears increasingly associated with satellite-cell participation.

### Decision rule
Satellite cells are part of the adaptive machinery, especially for substantial/long-term remodeling, but the coach should not use acute satellite-cell responses to select exercises or recovery intervals.

References: systematic review/meta-analysis PMID 36266373; human longitudinal study PMID 34264129; prolonged training study PMID 26407634; review PMID 34480776.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH** for participation in chronic adaptation; **LOW** for prescribing training from acute satellite-cell kinetics.

## 9. Neural adaptation and early strength gains

Strength is not muscle size. Resistance training produces neural adaptations involving motor-cortex/corticospinal inhibitory networks, motor-unit recruitment/coordination and task-specific skill. A systematic review/meta-analysis found strength training increased strength while reducing short-interval intracortical inhibition and cortical silent period, without consistent large changes in motor threshold.

Recent meta-analytic evidence also confirms that maximal-strength training changes both neural and muscular variables; the relative contribution varies with training status, task, contraction type and measurement method.

### Decision rule
A rapid increase in 1RM early in a program must not be interpreted as equivalent hypertrophy. For a performance goal, preserve task-specific practice; for a hypertrophy goal, evaluate muscle-size outcomes separately when measurement is justified.

References: PMID 28921683; PMID 40456806.

Confidence: **HIGH** that neural adaptation materially contributes to strength gains; **MODERATE** for quantifying its individual share without laboratory methods.

## 10. Fiber-type-specific hypertrophy

Type I and type II fibers can both hypertrophy with resistance training. Type II fibers often show larger absolute growth in traditional higher-load resistance training, but fiber-specific responses depend on population, protocol, effort and intervention. Low-load blood-flow-restriction training may produce relatively greater type I fiber hypertrophy, but direct comparative evidence is limited.

### Skill guardrail
Do not prescribe a specific load solely to “target slow-twitch” or “target fast-twitch hypertrophy.” Whole-muscle outcome evidence, strength specificity, fatigue cost and individual tolerance should dominate programming decisions.

Reference: fiber-type BFR systematic review PMID 37218848; broader mechanistic synthesis PMID 35389932.

Confidence: **MODERATE** for fiber-type differences; **LOW-MODERATE** for using them to personalize loading prescriptions.

## 11. Calcium handling and fatigue

Peripheral fatigue can involve reduced sarcoplasmic-reticulum Ca2+ release, altered myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity and reduced maximal cross-bridge force. Metabolic changes can contribute, and excitation-contraction coupling may become impaired during repeated/prolonged contractions. The exact contribution depends strongly on exercise mode, duration and fatigue state.

### Decision rule
Do not diagnose “calcium fatigue” from soreness, a poor workout or wearable data. Operationally classify the observed phenotype (loss of force/velocity, task failure, local fatigue, systemic fatigue) and manage training load/recovery; molecular attribution usually remains uncertain outside research settings.

Confidence: **HIGH** for the existence of Ca2+-handling contributions to peripheral fatigue; **LOW** for individual molecular inference in field coaching.

Reference foundation: classic EC-coupling fatigue review PMID 1313991 plus modern Ca2+-handling literature.

## 12. Detraining, retraining and muscle memory

Detraining reduces strength and muscle size, but they may decay at different rates and some adaptations persist. In older men, 12 weeks of detraining produced modest losses of strength/power, and less than 8 weeks of retraining were required to regain post-training 1RM levels in one study. A meta-analysis in middle-aged/older adults also found that previously gained strength can remain above pretraining levels after detraining, depending on prior training duration, intensity and detraining duration.

Human muscle-memory evidence now includes retained myonuclei after detraining and persistent epigenetic/proteomic signatures. A 2024 within-person human study found retained myonuclei after detraining and larger type-II fiber CSA in the previously trained limb after retraining, but the delta hypertrophic response was not clearly superior. A 2025 proteomic study found some resistance-training-induced proteins remained elevated after ~2.5 months without training. Therefore, cellular memory exists, but the practical magnitude of a “faster second gain” is not yet predictable for an individual.

### Decision rule
Do not assume complete reset after a training break. Re-entry can usually progress faster than a true novice program when symptoms, technique and tissue tolerance permit, but previous peak loads should not be restored automatically.

References: PMID 32017951; systematic review/meta-analysis PMID 34510028; human myonuclear study PMID 39159314; human proteomic memory PMID 40183698; systematic review/meta-analysis of myonuclear permanence PMID 35961635.

Confidence: **HIGH** that detraining causes partial loss and retraining restores performance; **MODERATE** that persistent cellular signatures contribute to human muscle memory; **LOW-MODERATE** for predicting individual reacquisition speed from those mechanisms.

## 13. Disuse atrophy is rapid and is not simply “more protein breakdown”

Immobilization/bed rest cause rapid declines in muscle size, strength and power. Human evidence indicates the early loss of function can exceed the loss of muscle size. Meta-analytic evidence shows disuse significantly lowers muscle protein synthesis; in uncomplicated disuse, reduced MPS appears to be a major driver of atrophy, while changes in protein breakdown are less consistently demonstrated in humans.

Protein/amino-acid supplementation alone has not reliably prevented immobilization-induced muscle atrophy in healthy adults, reinforcing that mechanical loading is a critical input.

### Decision rule
When safe and clinically appropriate, preserve mechanical stimulus and function during reduced activity rather than relying on nutrition alone. Exact rehabilitation loading belongs to the injury/tissue block and must respect medical restrictions.

References: MPS meta-analysis PMID 41166041; single-leg disuse meta-analysis PMID 36883219; human atrophy review PMID 38895777; protein/amino-acid meta-analysis PMID 38424716.

Confidence: **HIGH** that disuse rapidly reduces muscle function/size and depresses MPS; **MODERATE** for the relative contribution of each proteolytic pathway in vivo in humans.

## 14. Measurement: detect adaptation, not noise

### Strength
1RM testing generally has good-to-excellent test-retest reliability. A systematic review of 32 studies / 1595 participants reported median ICC 0.97 and median coefficient of variation ~4.2%, although individual protocols ranged wider (CV 0.5-12.1%). Reliability does not mean every small change is real.

### Operational rule
For a repeated standardized test, store its within-user variability. Prefer interpreting changes against the protocol's typical error / SEM / minimal detectable change rather than declaring every PR a physiological adaptation.

A useful hierarchy:
1. standardize equipment, ROM, technique, warm-up and timing;
2. obtain familiarization/baseline when needed;
3. track absolute result and percentage change;
4. maintain a rolling estimate of individual test variability when repeated often;
5. flag changes smaller than expected noise as **uncertain**, not improvement/decline.

### Hypertrophy
Imaging/ultrasound can be reliable with standardized landmarks and trained operators, but error depends on muscle, site, device and protocol. Consumer body-composition estimates should not be treated as equivalent to site-specific muscle imaging.

References: 1RM reliability systematic review PMID 32681399; athlete functional-test reliability systematic review PMID 40895367.

Confidence: **HIGH** for the need to model measurement error; **MODERATE** for portable cross-protocol thresholds because error is protocol-specific.

## 15. Human monitoring hooks for this block

### Minimal useful variables
- exercise / movement
- load
- reps
- sets
- RIR or RPE for selected work sets
- pain during and after training when relevant to programming
- next-session performance trend

### Optional higher-depth variables
- velocity for standardized lifts if a reliable device is available
- local soreness only when it changes programming or helps characterize a new response
- standardized circumference, ultrasound/MRI or body-composition measures when hypertrophy is a major goal and measurement quality is adequate
- periodic standardized 1RM/submaximal strength tests when the result changes programming

### Do not routinely collect
- soreness for its own sake
- consumer “muscle recovery” scores without demonstrated decision value
- acute molecular biomarkers in healthy recreational athletes
- genomic/rDNA measures to predict hypertrophy

### Individual-learning loop
For each repeated exercise pattern, estimate the relationship among dose (hard sets, load, RIR), acute cost (performance drop, soreness, fatigue), recovery time and subsequent performance/adaptation. Update recommendations from the person's own response history while constraining decisions by population evidence and safety.

## 16. Claims still pending / intentionally provisional

- relative importance of specific mechanosensors in vivo in humans
- whether ribosomal-biogenesis measures add actionable predictive value beyond ordinary training/performance data
- magnitude and timing of satellite-cell dependence in highly trained humans
- robust fiber-type-specific loading prescriptions
- practical field methods to partition central vs peripheral fatigue
- individualized models of muscle-memory/reacquisition speed
- protocol-specific SEM/MDC libraries for every strength, power and hypertrophy test used by Human Coach

These remain **PENDING/PROVISIONAL** and must not be converted into strong operational rules without stronger human longitudinal evidence.
